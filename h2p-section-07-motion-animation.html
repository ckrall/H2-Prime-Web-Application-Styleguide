Section 07 — Motion & Animation

Timing and easing tokens already existed in tokens.css §2.19 before this page — a full --duration-* and --ease-* scale with a working prefers-reduced-motion override. What didn't exist was a policy for when to reach for which value, or a single reference for what H2 Prime's motion is supposed to communicate. That's what this section is. See principle 08 on the cover page.

Why motion gets its own section
H2 Prime's audience skews older, works outdoors on a phone as often as at a desk, and is often mid-task under real time pressure — filing paperwork, not browsing. Motion here exists to answer "did that work" and "where did my thing go," not to feel modern. A transition that's decorative rather than informative is the wrong transition, full stop — see the anti-patterns in §7G.

7A

Motion principles

PrincipleIn practice
Motion signals, it doesn't decorateEvery transition on this page maps to a specific state change (§7D) or a specific enter/exit event (§7E). If a motion doesn't map to either, it doesn't ship.
Fast by defaultMost interactions use --duration-fast (100ms) or --duration-base (200ms). Nothing routine should feel like it's making the user wait on the interface.
Only transform and opacity animate by defaultBoth are compositor-only properties — no layout recalculation, no jank on a mid-range outdoor phone on a spotty connection. background-color and border-color are the two accepted exceptions, used for hover/focus color shifts.
Reduced motion is a floor, not an afterthoughtEvery duration token already collapses under prefers-reduced-motion: reduce (§7C) — new motion work should use the tokens specifically so this keeps working for free.

7C

Reduced motion policy

tokens.css already wraps every duration token in a prefers-reduced-motion: reduce override that zeroes --duration-fast, --duration-base, --duration-slow, and --duration-slower. Anything that animates purely through a token-driven CSS transition — which is everything in §7B and §7D — gets this for free.

@media (prefers-reduced-motion: reduce) {
  --duration-fast:   0ms;
  --duration-base:   0ms;
  --duration-slow:   0ms;
  --duration-slower: 0ms;
}
The token override doesn't reach everythingGAP
Two motion types in this section bypass the duration tokens entirely and need their own explicit prefers-reduced-motion handling, written per-component rather than inherited automatically: CSS @keyframes animations (the spinner and skeleton loader in §6H — both already carry a manual override in their own CSS, but that pattern needs to be documented as the rule, not a one-off), and any future JavaScript-driven animation (a custom drag interaction, a canvas-based chart transition) that doesn't read CSS custom properties at all. Until a written rule exists, treat "does this respect reduced motion" as a required manual check for anything outside a plain CSS transition.
Don't just disable motion — some still needs to complete

A loading spinner that stops entirely under reduced motion leaves no indication anything is happening. The spinner in §6H slows to 1.4s instead of stopping, so state is still communicated without the vestibular trigger of fast continuous rotation.

Do cut duration and distance, not meaning

A card that lifts 3px on hover in 200ms can drop straight to its end state in 0ms under reduced motion — the state change (hovered) is still communicated, just without the tween.

Three of the four surfaces above (toast, dropdown, modal) don't exist as components anywhere in the system yet — Section 5 stops at buttons, forms, badges, cards, tables, and banners. The timings here are reasonable defaults drawn from the existing duration scale, offered so motion isn't invented from scratch when those components get built, but they should be revisited against a real toast/dropdown/modal spec rather than treated as settled. 7F

Phase & progress motionGAP

The Contract Phase System (Section 10) is parked pending revalidation against the Brown/Neutral revision, so nothing here can define how a phase transition animates — a progress-bar fill, a step-indicator advance — without guessing at a visual system that isn't settled yet. This is intentionally left open rather than speculatively designed against a palette likely to change.

Placeholder decision, once Section 10 lands
Whatever the phase indicator ends up looking like, its transition between phases should very likely use --duration-slow (300ms) and --ease-default — phase changes are infrequent, meaningful events, not routine hover feedback, and deserve to read as more deliberate than a button press. That's a placeholder, not a decision — revisit once Section 10 actually has a visual design to attach it to.
7G

Anti-patterns

Don't animate width, height, top, or left

These trigger layout recalculation on every frame. On a mid-range phone in direct sunlight with a weak connection — the exact H2 Prime mobile use case — that shows up as visible stutter. Use transform: translate() or transform: scale() instead; both are compositor-only.

Do animate transform and opacity

Every demo on this page sticks to these two (plus color, for hover/focus feedback) specifically so it stays smooth on the hardware the audience is actually using.

Don't autoplay carousels or looping animation

Continuous motion competes for attention on a screen someone is glancing at outdoors between other tasks, drains battery, and — if it can't be paused — fails WCAG 2.2.2 (Pause, Stop, Hide) outright.

Do keep motion event-driven

Every pattern in §7D–§7E fires because the user did something (hovered, clicked, triggered a state change) or the system has something to report (a banner appearing). Nothing on this page animates on a timer.

Don't communicate a state change through motion alone

A card that only signals "selected" by animating into a slightly different position, with no color or icon change, is invisible to anyone who missed the transition — including a screen reader user, for whom the animation never existed.

Do pair motion with a persistent state change

The card hover-lift in §7D moves and adds a shadow that stays for as long as the hover holds — the shadow, not the movement, is what actually communicates the state once the animation settles.
