H2 Prime Web Application Design System & Guidelines

Section 14 — Overlay & Surface System

Six surfaces that sit above the page — modal, drawer, sheet, popover, toast, inline expansion — and one rule for choosing between them. Written because the product has been through two opposite answers to the same question: the original designer put every side action in a sliding panel, and that was reversed to put every side action in a modal. Both are right some of the time. This section says when, and why, and what makes a panel legible on a phone.

14A

The problem this section exists to settle

Side actions in H2 Prime were originally handled with sliding panels. The implementation was poor and the pattern was unintuitive, and the response was to move every side-action workflow to a modal. That was an improvement — but it was an improvement for a reason worth naming, because the notification centre now brings panels back and the reason determines whether that is a mistake or not.

The specific complaint about panels was about mobile: a panel takes the whole screen, nothing distinguishes it from a page or from another route, and the user loses track of where they are. That complaint is correct, and it is not a complaint about panels. It is a complaint about a panel that stops behaving like a panel. Section 14D deals with it directly.

Why sort by shape at all

Overlay systems are usually catalogued by shape — here is our modal, here is our drawer. Shape is the output. Sorting by shape is what produces a system where the choice between two components is a matter of taste, which is exactly how a product ends up with every side action in a panel one year and every side action in a modal the next.

14B

The rule

What actually differs between overlay surfaces is the relationship between what is on top and what is underneath. One question settles it.

Is the page underneath still the user's place?
NO
They have left it. They cannot continue until this is dealt with, and the page behind is irrelevant while it is open. → Modal. Centred, heavy scrim, two explicit outcomes.
YES
They are still standing there. Something arrived alongside. They may read it, act on it, or abandon it, and either way they are coming back. → Drawer on a wide screen, sheet on a narrow one. Edge-anchored, lighter scrim, one way out.

This is why moving the side-action workflows to modals was right. Request help, add a document, edit a note, generate a shipping label — every one is a self-contained task with a commit and a cancel. Nothing on the page behind is needed to complete it. They were relationship-one content wearing a relationship-two shape, and a panel that demands completion while looking like it does not is exactly as confusing as it sounds. No amount of polish on the panel implementation would have fixed that.

It is also why the notification centre is genuinely different. Nobody completes their notifications. They glance, perhaps follow one, and return to the contract they were working on. The page behind is their place, and keeping it visible is the entire point of the surface.

ADR 14-1 — The trace rule

Every overlay must leave a visible trace of the surface it came from. A modal leaves it through the scrim; a drawer leaves it beside; a sheet leaves it above. If a surface cannot leave a trace, because it covers the whole viewport, it is not an overlay — it is a page, and it belongs behind a route with a URL and a back button. This is the constraint that resolves the mobile panel problem, and it also catches a second failure from the opposite direction: a modal sized to 90% of a 1512px viewport is in the same trouble.

14D

Making a panel legible on a phone

This is the part that failed before. On a 390px screen a 460px drawer cannot sit beside anything, so it becomes a sheet — and the moment a sheet covers the viewport, the signal that said “you are still here” is gone. A full-bleed panel, a modal and a new route all look identical. That is precisely the disorientation that got panels banned.

Four cues restore it. They are not equally important, and saying which does the work matters more than listing them.

The peek load-bearing — 56px
The sheet stops short of the top and the page behind stays visible. This is the one that matters; switch it off and the other three cannot save it, because there is no longer a page to point at. 56px is one row of the page behind — enough to be recognisable rather than read as a gap. Token: --overlay-sheet-peek.
A translucent scrim load-bearing
The peek only works if you can see through to it. An opaque backdrop is a page by another name. The drawer and sheet scrim is deliberately lighter than the modal scrim — 0.45 against 0.60 — because legibility of the thing behind is the point rather than a side effect. Token: --scrim-drawer.
The page recedes supporting
The content behind scales back slightly and rounds its corners, which reads as depth rather than replacement. Pleasant and worth having; not what is holding the pattern up.
A grabber supporting
A handle is a thing that sits on top of another thing. Screens do not have handles. Cheap, and it advertises drag-to-dismiss — which must never be the only way out; the close button stays. Tokens: --overlay-grabber-width, --overlay-grabber-height.
Do

Let the sheet stop at the peek, always, at every height.

Keep the scrim translucent enough to read the page through.

Route to a page when the content genuinely needs the whole screen.

Don't

Let a sheet expand to full height on drag. At that point it is a page pretending.

Use an opaque backdrop to “focus” the panel. It removes the only orientation cue.

Ship drag-to-dismiss without a close button.

ADR 14-2 — Answering the mobile question

Yes, a panel can be made obvious on a phone — but only by refusing to let it fill the screen. If the content needs the full viewport, that is the system saying it was never a panel. The previous implementation was not fixable by better animation or better styling; it was a page that had been given a panel's affordances, and no cue survives that.

14E

Motion is the grammar

Can modal and panel animations be mixed without it feeling arbitrary? The answer is sharper than yes: they must differ. The direction a surface arrives from is the fastest signal the user gets about what kind of surface it is, and giving everything the same transition throws that signal away.

What keeps mixing from feeling arbitrary is not using fewer animations. It is a single rule: the same kind of content always arrives the same way. A drawer always comes from the trailing edge. A sheet always comes from the bottom. A modal never slides at all — it rises eight pixels and fades, because a lift is not a journey and a modal has not taken you anywhere.

Exits are faster than entrances, deliberately

Entering is where a surface has to explain itself, so it gets 200–300ms. Leaving is a decision already made, so it gets 100–200ms — waiting for a panel you have already dismissed is the single thing that makes an interface feel slow. Tokens: --duration-modal-in / -out, --duration-drawer-in / -out, --duration-sheet-in / -out.

Reduced motion

Under prefers-reduced-motion: reduce, every overlay duration collapses to 0ms and the modal's rise distance to 0 — handled once in tokens.css §2.22, never per component. The surfaces still read as different, because position and scrim carry the meaning even with the motion gone. That is the test of whether the grammar is real: if removing the animation makes two surfaces indistinguishable, the animation was doing work the layout should have been doing.

14F

Nesting

Overlay systems rot through nesting, so the rule is short.

Nesting rules
Inside a…AllowedNot allowed
ModalPopover, toast, inline expansionAnother modal · a drawer · a sheet
Drawer / sheetPopover, toast, inline expansion, and one confirmation modalA second drawer · a second sheet
PopoverNothingEverything

A confirmation modal over a drawer is allowed because the two are different kinds — the drawer accompanies, the modal interrupts — so the stack still reads correctly: the modal sits above, the drawer stays put, and closing the modal returns you to the drawer rather than to the page. A modal opening a modal is always a sign the first one was doing two jobs; split it or route it.

One practical consequence for the notification centre: deleting a notification uses a toast with undo rather than a confirmation modal. Not because the nesting would break, but because a modal would interrupt a glance — and the deleted item is recoverable for thirty days regardless.

14G

Accessibility model

Behaviour follows from the surface, not from the component. These are not per-component choices, and a component that departs from them is wrong rather than special.

Prefer the platform

<dialog> with showModal() provides the focus trap, Escape handling and an inert background without a line of script, and the top layer removes z-index fights entirely. Hand-rolled focus traps are a recurring source of defects; reach for one only when the surface genuinely cannot be a dialog.

The defect that is invisible until someone tabs

Focus must return to the trigger on every close path — Escape, the close button, a scrim click, the confirm button, and the case where the thing you were looking at no longer exists. Missing one path is the most common overlay bug in any system and it never shows up in a visual review.

A panel that leaves the page usable is a different component

If a surface carries a scrim and makes the background inert, it is modal and must behave modally. A genuinely non-modal inspector panel — no scrim, no trap, page still editable — is a separate component and is deliberately not in this system yet. Add one when a real workflow needs it, not in anticipation.

ADR 14-3 — Two title roles, not one

--type-modal-title-* is Barlow Semi Condensed at 26px; --type-overlay-title-* is Inter semibold at 22px. The difference is not decoration. A modal announces itself and borrows the display voice to do it; a drawer is a companion to the page and takes the quieter body-family heading. Typeface is one more thing telling the user which kind of surface they are looking at, and it keeps working when the motion has been turned off.
